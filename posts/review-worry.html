LONOWN & riserayss's "worry" Bounced Angelcore Back Into the Scene Full Swing

A review of the late-2025 release that, through a single Mariah Carey interpolation and a slowed-version distribution that traveled across nearly every working angelcore curation, did more to re-energize the scene than any single track since Sleepwalker.

by undrwrld_rcrds∙Review

By late 2025 the angelcore scene was, by most working measures, in a slow stretch. The genre's foundational records had been established and recirculated past the point of newness; the canonical producers' release calendars had quieted somewhat after the Fake Everything cycle; the broader playlist apparatus was sustaining itself on slowed-version reissues of catalog rather than on fresh material that demanded attention. None of this constituted a crisis — the scene's underlying infrastructure was healthy — but it did mean that the genre had reached the kind of consolidation plateau where one more reliable mid-tempo angelcore track was not going to move anything.

And then LONOWN and riserayss released worry.

The Mariah Carey Move

The track's organizing decision is the sample. worry interpolates Mariah Carey's Underneath the Stars — a 1995 b-side that most listeners under thirty have never heard, processed through the riserayss vocal treatment until the source is unrecognizable as Mariah and recognizable instead as exactly the kind of pitched, dissolved vocal element that the genre's vocabulary has been built on. The interpolation is doing several things at once. It pulls a piece of 1990s commercial pop into the angelcore frame in a way that resonates with the broader scene's recombinant instincts; it gives the riserayss performance a melodic foundation that the genre's standard pad-and-noise palette would not otherwise have provided; and it functions as the kind of half-buried citation that listeners who catch it will remember and listeners who don't will simply hear as good production.

What LONOWN does around the sample is more interesting than the sample itself. There's a synth bass that enters in the second half — low enough that you feel it before you hear it, mixed quiet enough that it never announces itself — that effectively replaces the kick drum the genre would normally use here. The percussion is there but it doesn't drive. The arc of the song builds toward a chorus that the production then refuses to deliver, leaving the Mariah Carey ghost hanging in the air. By the time the track has run its full 3:21, the listener has been inside something that feels both immediately familiar and unmistakably newer than the rest of the contemporary catalog.

The track is what happens when a scene's most reliable producers stop trying to surprise and start trying to refine. The refinement is the surprise.

The Distribution and the Bounce

What turned worry from a strong release into a scene-defining one was the distribution. The track was issued in original, slowed, and ultra-slowed versions in close sequence — the parallel-version model the scene has fully operationalized — and within weeks of the release, worry (Slowed) was placed at or near the opening cut on essentially every working angelcore curation. The prodvnxious-curated YouTube mix opens with it. The ANGELCORE.wav vol.3 playlist places it second, immediately after plaguedoll and bulletrain's Drain. Entity Music's Angelcore curation features the slowed version on its top track row. The Spotify scene-canonical playlists all moved worry into rotation within days of the slowed release.

The cumulative effect is that, for the period from roughly late October 2025 through early 2026, worry functioned as the genre's most-encountered new track. Listeners arriving at the scene through any of the major curation entry points were almost guaranteed to hear it; listeners working through the scene's broader catalog kept encountering it across multiple parallel placements. The track did not need to chart commercially to do its scene-internal work — it accumulated the kind of curatorial presence that produces durable attention, the way a record that lands on every reliable playlist becomes part of the genre's working memory for years afterward.

What the Track Did for the Scene

The structural value of worry — beyond the track itself, which is excellent — is that it demonstrated something the genre needed to demonstrate at that moment in its trajectory. Specifically: that the established producers could still release into the scene's current configuration without the result reading as either calcified self-imitation or as a deviation that breaks the genre's working pact with its listeners. worry is recognizably an angelcore record. It runs the moves the scene has formalized. It does not announce any new direction. And it lands harder than most of what the scene had produced in the previous six months, on the strength of execution rather than novelty.

This matters because most internet-native microgenres do not survive the consolidation plateau. The producers who built the foundational sound get tired of working in it, or the scene's listeners get tired of hearing the same moves run, or the broader streaming ecology moves on to whatever new aesthetic the algorithm starts surfacing. worry demonstrated that angelcore could, instead, refine in place — producing tracks that work at a craft level the foundational records did not quite achieve, and doing so without breaking the genre's vocabulary. Whether the scene capitalizes on the demonstration is a different question; what is certain is that worry bought the scene some additional working room to figure out what comes next.
