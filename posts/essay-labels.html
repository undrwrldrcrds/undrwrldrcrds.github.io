The Labels Building Angelcore: ARCADIA / IMMINENT, Azurite, EPSILE, NCS and the Major-Label Test

On the labels powering the contemporary scene — from ARCADIA / IMMINENT as the original angelcore label apparatus to Azurite, EPSILE, and the larger copyright-free platforms that have started releasing witch house tracks alongside the dedicated angelcore imprints.

by undrwrld_rcrds∙Scene Infrastructure

The contemporary angelcore scene has, somewhat unusually for an internet-native microgenre, developed a tiered label ecosystem rather than consolidating around a single dominant imprint. Four distinct categories of label operate inside or adjacent to the scene as of mid-2026, and the interplay between them is doing more work for the genre's health than any single one of them could do alone. This is the working map.

Tier 1: The Dedicated Angelcore Labels

Three independent labels function as the scene's editorial backbone. All are working at the intersection of release calendar and curatorial practice; all have built infrastructure specifically tuned to the parallel-version, playlist-driven distribution model the scene runs on; and the three of them together cover most of the dedicated angelcore release calendar in 2026.

Azurite Records, founded and operated by MitroWave, is the larger of the two by visible metrics. By the standard label-tracking measures, the operation is substantial: 641 documented credits, 159 separate artist profiles, the kind of release cadence that has placed the imprint in the top 0.1% of labels by activity per muso.ai. Beyond the release calendar, Azurite runs a parallel editorial layer through two Spotify playlists — the main Angelcore curation (148 tracks, 91.6K saves at time of writing) and the rolling ANGELCORE OF THE WEEK selection — that together function as one of the scene's most reliable producer-discovery pipelines. Crisys, V.I.P.N, fervnt, ENXK, lxst child, and dozens of other satellite-tier producers have moved through the Azurite release calendar; Crisys's knightslayer (September 2025) is one of the clearer recent demonstrations of how the label uses single-track releases to build sustained playlist presence for its roster.

EPSILE Records, the project of producer SPYRAL and based in Germany, occupies a tighter but no less interesting niche. The label's positioning per its own framing covers "Phonk and Funk to Dark Electronic and Experimental" — a broader stylistic scope than Azurite's tighter angelcore-and-adjacent focus, but with substantial overlap on the contemporary scene's working aesthetic. The release model leans toward EP-length curated drops rather than rolling single-track distribution, and the demo-submissions inbox (demo@epsilerecords.com) operates more like a traditional indie label A&R pipeline.

Cresta La Cultura, the third dedicated label, operates as a self-described "international music label and creative collective" — a framing that, in practice, translates to the most catalog-deep and roster-broad of the three. The label's own metrics, per its website, claim 500+ artists, 1,500+ releases, and over 4 billion cumulative streams across platforms — an order of magnitude larger than either Azurite or EPSILE on raw release-volume terms. Beyond the scale, the structural distinction is the curatorial scope: Cresta La Cultura runs the broadest stylistic remit of the three dedicated labels, with releases spanning angelcore, phonk, hardwave, dark electronic, and the broader contemporary underground dance ecology. Within the angelcore corner specifically, the label hosts releases from akiaura / LONOWN / DJ Pointless (XVI), DJ Anemia with crier (stacy) and with DVRKLXGHT (silkgrave), Crisys (clarity with Senkya), angelrot (Mira with DJ PLAYSTATION), and the broader satellite roster. The instagram presence (@crestalacultura, 51K followers) and the demo inbox (crestalacultura@gmail.com) operate as the label's main producer-facing infrastructure. Per Urban Dictionary's user-written definition of angelcore, Cresta La Cultura and ARCADIA / IMMINENT are co-credited as the labels that "started and currently market" the genre — a framing that gives Cresta La Cultura more historical weight than its release volume alone would suggest.

The most useful single artifact for understanding how Azurite and EPSILE relate is the Angelcore: Compiled compilation — a project that pulled producers from both rosters into a shared canonical document and that has, since its release, functioned as one of the genre's most-cited entry points for new listeners. The three-label relationship more broadly is less of a competitive arrangement than a complementary division of labor: Azurite handles the high-cadence playlist apparatus and the dedicated angelcore curation work; EPSILE handles the curated EP-cycle releases at the tighter dark-electronic end; Cresta La Cultura handles the broadest stylistic and roster scope, functioning as a kind of catalog-deep home that producers move through alongside their work on the more focused labels. Most working angelcore producers operate across at least two of the three; the multi-label release pattern is the rule rather than the exception.

Tier 2: ARCADIA / IMMINENT — The Original Angelcore Label

ARCADIA and its affiliated imprint IMMINENT are, by most working measures, the original angelcore label apparatus and the one most directly responsible for the genre as a coherent scene. Treated by Spotify as effectively a single entity and credited interchangeably across releases (the Deathwish single is credited to "virtual memory404 / ARCADIA," the *starly (Ultra Slowed)* release reads "© 2025 ARCADIA," and most of the akiaura / LONOWN canonical-tier discography moves through this combined apparatus), ARCADIA / IMMINENT is where the genre's foundational records were released and where the working canon continues to be set in 2026. Coverage of the apparatus is split across a separate dedicated essay in this publication and the broader scene history writeups; the short version is that nearly every track most listeners would name as foundational to the genre — Sleepwalker, Destruction Age, archangel, starly, worry — moved through this label apparatus on its way into the broader scene.

What's distinctive about ARCADIA / IMMINENT as a label is that it has continued to evolve in real time rather than calcifying around its founding catalog. The 2025–2026 release calendar — the *Fake Everything* and *Fake Everything (Slowed)* sequence, the *worry* parallel-version distribution, the ongoing LONOWN solo catalog, the new *Feel My Heart* and *addiction* singles — shows a label apparatus that is still actively shaping the genre rather than just maintaining its existing roster. This is, structurally, what makes the scene's overall configuration sustainable: the canonical-tier label is not a museum piece, and producers releasing through ARCADIA / IMMINENT in 2026 are doing work that materially adds to the genre's working catalog rather than just decorating it.

The relationship between ARCADIA / IMMINENT and the dedicated indies above (Azurite, EPSILE, Cresta La Cultura) is one of mutual reinforcement: the canonical-center releases set the scene's working vocabulary and create the gravity that pulls listeners into the broader ecosystem, while the dedicated indies populate the long tail with producers who learn that vocabulary and build their own catalogs inside it. Without the canonical center, the satellite labels would not have a reliable working aesthetic to organize around; without the satellite labels, ARCADIA / IMMINENT would not have the depth of catalog that lets the scene sustain repeated listening past the initial canonical encounter.

Tier 3: The Adjacent Indie Ecosystem

A loose ecosystem of smaller imprints rounds out the working label tier — S4NCTUARY (handling thematic curations adjacent to the main scene), various single-release Bandcamp operations run by individual producers, and the broader playlist-curator ecosystem that operates at the boundary between label and editorial publication. Most of these operations are not labels in the traditional sense — they don't sign producers, don't handle marketing, don't run release calendars in any formalized way — but together they provide the connective tissue that lets producers move between the satellite tier and the canonical center without needing to be picked up by a single dominant home.

For producers releasing across the scene, the practical pattern that has emerged is multi-label: most working angelcore producers have releases across two or three of the above tiers rather than committing to a single home. The angelrot catalog, to take one example, has moved across Azurite and EPSILE alongside independent self-releases; the Crisys catalog has Azurite at its center but also moves through other curatorial homes; most producers in the broader roster operate in similarly distributed fashion. This multi-home pattern is the scene's working answer to the structural question of how an internet-native microgenre maintains catalog diversity without fracturing into incompatible roster blocks.

Tier 4: The Major-Adjacent Test

The development worth watching most closely, as of late 2025 and early 2026, is the appearance of witch-house and angelcore-adjacent material on labels much larger than the dedicated indies discussed above.

NCS (NoCopyrightSounds), the YouTube-native copyright-free music apparatus that has become one of the largest electronic-music release platforms by volume, has been issuing tracks explicitly tagged as Witch House at a sustained cadence through 2025 and into 2026. The catalog is broader than just witch house — NCS releases across phonk, hyperpop, alternative dance, and most adjacent electronic registers — but the witch-house tag in particular has accumulated enough working releases that it has become a recognizable corner of the platform. Crisys's FATE (February 2026, tagged Witch House on the NCS release) and the TWISTED / Crisys / ProdChxn / glossier collaboration LUSTER (also February 2026, Witch House) are two of the clearer demonstrations that producers operating in the contemporary scene are also working through the NCS apparatus when the release format fits. KREZUS & Surreal_dvd, Aditya Sharma, criticaleye, SHIRO, EVILDXER, kellapsage, and Rameses B have all appeared on the NCS witch-house calendar across the same period.

What's interesting structurally about NCS doing this is the scale mismatch. NCS operates orders of magnitude larger than any of the dedicated indies covered above — the platform's main release calendar reaches audiences in the tens of millions across YouTube and adjacent video platforms — and a single NCS release of an angelcore-adjacent track can deliver discovery exposure that no dedicated indie can match. The tradeoff is that NCS is not a curatorial home in the way Azurite or EPSILE is: tracks released through NCS sit alongside genre-incompatible work, get used as background music in creator videos, and operate inside the platform's broader "music for content" economy rather than inside a scene-specific editorial frame. Producers releasing through NCS are doing it for exposure, not for editorial placement, and the tradeoffs reflect that.

Monstercat, the larger Canadian electronic-music label most known for its melodic dubstep, drum & bass, and progressive house catalog, has been exploring witch-house-adjacent territory more cautiously. The label has not built a dedicated witch-house release pipeline in the way NCS has, but releases pulling from the broader dark-electronic and atmospheric-edge palettes have appeared periodically across the catalog. Whether this exploration crystallizes into a sustained presence in the angelcore-adjacent space is the open question; the label has the infrastructure to do it if the audience pulls hard enough, but does not currently appear committed to the experiment at the same depth NCS has demonstrated.

The Sample Pack and Tutorial Ecosystem

One useful indicator of where a microgenre sits on the maturity curve is whether anyone is selling sample packs and production tutorials targeting it. By that measure, angelcore has crossed the threshold. KVASS LAB, a sample-pack vendor based in the broader contemporary electronic production ecosystem, sells an "Angelcore Sample Pack (akiaura, LONOWN, DJ Anemia)" — 530+ drum loops, melodic loops, FX, and processed vocal stems explicitly marketed as the building blocks of the canonical-tier sound. The pack is one of the vendor's main contemporary listings and sits alongside production-tutorial offerings that walk producers through how to construct the genre's signature lead sounds, pad arrangements, and percussion programming. Electronic Samples, a separate vendor in the same ecosystem, offers a similar "Angelcore [Akiaura x Lonown Style] Tutorial Files + Template" package targeted at producers trying to replicate the canonical-tier production approach.

What this commercial layer represents is, structurally, the genre crossing from "underground microscene" to "established production register" — the point at which the genre's working aesthetic has solidified enough that other producers want to learn how to make tracks in its style, and at which sample-pack vendors have identified enough commercial demand to invest in producing dedicated educational and material assets. Most internet-native microgenres do not reach this threshold; the ones that do tend to be the ones that survive past the founding cohort's individual release calendars and develop into broader production traditions that other producers operate inside.

The presence of this ecosystem is, by some measure, more reliable evidence of the genre's medium-term viability than the streaming numbers on the canonical releases. Streaming numbers reflect listener attention, which is cyclical and partially algorithmic; sample packs reflect producer attention, which is cumulative and indicates that the genre has become a working compositional resource for the next generation of releases. A genre with sample packs available is a genre that other producers will be operating inside for the foreseeable future.

What This Means for the Scene

The interesting structural fact about the contemporary angelcore label ecosystem is that it has, more or less in one go, produced both dedicated indie infrastructure and major-label-adjacent exposure pathways. Most internet-native microgenres develop one or the other, not both at once. Hyperpop got the dedicated indies first (PC Music and adjacent) and the major-label exposure later (the Charli XCX / Sophie crossover sequence); witch house got dedicated indies (Tri Angle, Disaro) but never the major-label exposure pathway, which is part of why it faded as a coherent scene in the mid-2010s. Angelcore has, by mid-2026, somehow ended up with both — independent labels handling the editorial work, plus larger platforms providing exposure scaffolding for the producers willing to release into them.

Whether the configuration sustains as the scene scales is the question worth asking. The dedicated indies have to keep doing their curatorial work without getting overwhelmed by major-platform attention; the major platforms have to keep enough room for the scene's actual aesthetic without smoothing it into something more algorithm-friendly; the producers operating across both have to keep the catalog coherent across releases that target different audiences. None of this is guaranteed to work. But the fact that all of it is happening at once is, by some measure, the healthiest structural position any internet-native microgenre has occupied in the streaming era.

For Producers

The practical breakdown for producers working in or near the angelcore aesthetic and looking for label placement, in rough order of barrier-to-entry:

  • Azurite Records (azuriterecordsdemo@gmail.com / ko-fi submission). Best for producers working specifically in the angelcore / synthwave palette and looking for ongoing playlist editorial support.
  • EPSILE (demo@epsilerecords.com). Best for producers with EP-length material in the broader dark electronic / phonk / experimental space looking for a traditional label release structure.
  • NCS. Best for producers willing to work inside the platform's broader content-music economy in exchange for substantially larger exposure reach. Submission process via the NCS site; competitive but achievable for solid contemporary witch-house material.
  • The canonical-center apparatus. Less formal submissions process; placement is largely relational, driven by the broader scene collaboration network. Difficult to enter cold; useful to be aware of as the canonical-tier target.
