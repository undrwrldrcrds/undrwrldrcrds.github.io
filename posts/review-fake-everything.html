Fake Everything Is the akiaura / LONOWN Collaboration at Full Confidence

A full review of the 2025 third-collaboration release — the record where the genre's working vocabulary settles into its mature configuration, and where the producers stop having to demonstrate what they can do.

by undrwrld_rcrds∙Album Review

Fake Everything arrived in 2025 as the third collaborative full-length from akiaura and LONOWN, following Ketamine Girl (2023) and Destruction Age (2024). The release sequence matters for how the record is read. Ketamine Girl was the producers' working partnership introducing itself; Destruction Age was the partnership demonstrating that the underlying sound could sustain album-length attention; Fake Everything is the partnership having nothing left to prove. The result is, on first listen, the least dramatic of the three records — and on repeated listening, the one most cleanly inhabiting the genre's mature working register.

What Settling In Sounds Like

The most useful framing for Fake Everything is that it is the record where the producers stop having to demonstrate what they can do and start working inside what they have already established. The witch-house DNA is still present in the low end and in the more aggressive moments; the pad-forward atmosphere that defined Destruction Age's longer stretches is still doing structural work; the parallel-version distribution model still operates around the release. What has changed is the relative weighting. The album sounds less like a project arguing for the existence of angelcore as a coherent genre, and more like a project working inside angelcore as an already-established register.

This is, by some distance, the structurally correct move at this point in the producers' trajectory. Destruction Age did the work of establishing what the genre could sound like at album length; Fake Everything demonstrates that the producers can return to the established working vocabulary without it feeling repetitive. The individual tracks are not, on balance, more inventive than what's on Destruction Age — they are more confident. Same difference, in some real sense, but the confidence is what matters at this point in the catalog.

The Slowed Edition

The release was followed in 2026 by a full Fake Everything (Slowed) edition — the parallel-version distribution model applied at album length rather than just to individual canonical tracks. This is a interesting choice. Most of the scene's parallel-version distribution operates at the single-track level (each canonical track issued in original / slowed / ultra-slowed forms as independent releases); applying the model to a full album reframes the entire project as having two equally legitimate versions rather than one canonical version and a derivative slowed edition.

The slowed edition also demonstrates one of the genre's more interesting working features: the songs work in both forms. Most pop and dance music is, structurally, designed for a specific tempo — slowing it down by 15–20% breaks the rhythmic arrangements, exposes the production choices that were tempo-dependent, and produces something that sounds noticeably worse than the original. Angelcore records, by structural design, tend to survive the slowing process intact. Fake Everything (Slowed) is a useful single document for understanding why: the original record's compositional choices already accommodate the slower register, and the slowed edition reads more as a parallel presentation of the same material than as a derivative.

The Individual Tracks

The album's working sequence operates as a series of variations on the established LONOWN / akiaura working method rather than as a structural argument with a clear narrative arc. This is the album's distinctive choice — most album-length releases, including Destruction Age, operate with an audible structural progression across the runtime; Fake Everything mostly does not. The tracks function more as individually-legible cuts that happen to share an album frame than as a single sustained statement.

The Deathwish single (the Baby Jane / akiaura / LONOWN collaboration released in April 2025) sits at the more vocally-forward end of the album's working range, and is the cut most accessible to listeners arriving from outside the genre. The track is also, by streaming metrics, one of the album's stronger commercial performers — the vocal-forward arrangement does what the genre's more dissolved atmospheric tracks structurally do not, which is to provide a single-listen emotional payoff that can land before the listener has accumulated genre-specific listening experience.

The album's harder cuts are where akiaura is most audible — louder kicks, heavier saturation on the bass, the kind of percussion programming that you'd associate with witch house if the rest of the track wasn't telling you this is angelcore. The softer cuts are where LONOWN is doing most of the work. There's a difference between a producer making a quiet track because they ran out of louder ideas and a producer making a quiet track on purpose, and LONOWN's quieter writing always sounds like the latter — chord changes that arrive a half-bar later than you'd expect, melodies that resolve to the wrong note and then sit there.

The album is the partnership operating without needing to surprise either itself or its audience. The lack of surprise is the achievement.

What This Means for the Scene

The structural value of Fake Everything, beyond the record itself, is that it demonstrates the genre can sustain mature-phase records that work at craft level rather than at announcement level. Most internet-native microgenres struggle with the transition from "what the genre is" records to "what the genre's working producers do now that the genre exists" records — the audience that arrived for the foundational documents often disperses when the foundational documents stop being the point. Fake Everything shows the genre surviving that transition; the record is recognizably angelcore without needing to announce itself as such, and the audience has stayed with the producers through the transition.

Whether the scene at large makes the same transition is a separate question. The dedicated indies (Azurite, EPSILE) are still operating in a high-cadence, announcement-driven mode; most of the satellite tier is still producing tracks that read as scene-statement records rather than as catalog-builders. Fake Everything suggests that the canonical-tier producers have already moved past that mode; the rest of the scene's medium-term health depends on whether the broader tier can do the same.

Where to Start

  • Deathwish (with Baby Jane). The most accessible single-track entry into the album. Useful for listeners coming from outside the genre.
  • The original full-length, in sequence. The album is structurally designed for sustained listening rather than for single-track extraction.
  • The Fake Everything (Slowed) edition. Useful as a second pass — same material, different tempo, the differences are instructive.
