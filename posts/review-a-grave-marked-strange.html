Baby Jane's A Grave Marked Strange Is Gothic House Arriving at Album Length

A full review of the 2025 Baby Jane debut album — where the producer-singer-songwriter's self-described 'gothic house' register, previously demonstrated across singles and the Deathwish collaboration, settles into its first sustained album-length statement.

by undrwrld_rcrds∙Album Review

Baby Jane — Sofia Bella Galuz, born 2001 in Oakland to Soviet immigrant parents, based in Los Angeles since adolescence — arrived at A Grave Marked Strange in June 2025 after roughly three years of building a singles-driven catalog across the alt-pop and electronic adjacencies. The album is the producer-singer-songwriter's first sustained full-length statement, and it is also the document where her self-described "gothic house" register — previously demonstrated in scattered fragments across the singles catalog and most visibly in the Deathwish collaboration with akiaura and LONOWN — settles into a coherent album-length working project.

The Gothic House Position

"Gothic house" is, by the producer's own framing, a fusion of witch house, hardstyle, ethereal pop, and what she describes as the cinematic atmospheres of the underground LA DJing scene she came up through in the post-COVID era. The terminology is the producer's own, and the genre frame is partially aspirational — gothic house is not a recognized scene in the way angelcore or aero is, and the term reads more as a working framework for an individual project than as a description of an existing community of producers. What the framing does usefully accomplish is to position the project at a specific intersection: harder than the dreampop-adjacent satellite tier of angelcore, softer than the witch-house lineage proper, vocally-forward in a way that distinguishes the project from both reference points.

A Grave Marked Strange is the document that argues for this position. The album's working palette pulls from all three reference points the producer cites: the witch-house DNA is audible in the noise floor and in the harder tracks' percussion programming; the hardstyle influence shows up in the more aggressive moments' kick programming; the ethereal pop sensibility carries the vocal performances, which are forward in the mix and structurally central to most of the album's tracks.

The Vocal Project

The structural choice that distinguishes A Grave Marked Strange from most contemporary electronic releases is the centrality of the vocal performance. Most angelcore and adjacent records treat vocal contributions as texture — pitched samples, dissolved fragments, the kind of arrangements that operate at the boundary between music and atmosphere. A Grave Marked Strange reverses this. The vocal performance is the album's primary structural element; the electronic production is doing supporting work around it.

This is, by some distance, the album's most consequential choice. The producer's vocal performance is legible, emotionally direct, and structurally central to most of the record's tracks — which is structurally closer to alt-pop than to the genre's broader working consensus. What the production adds to the alt-pop base is the textural density and atmospheric darkness that the witch-house and hardstyle reference points contribute. The result is an album that reads as legitimately hybrid: alt-pop arrangement choices delivered through dark-electronic production craft.

Most contemporary electronic albums treat the vocal as texture. This album treats the production as texture and lets the voice carry the structural argument. The reversal is the album's defining choice.

The Track Sequence

The album includes the singles Eternal Embrace, Psychotrance, Cemetery Date, End of the Night, alongside the deeper album cuts that develop the working register. The sequencing operates as a series of variations on the same core emotional terrain — devotion, desperation, occult imagery, the kind of dark-romantic territory that the producer's lyric content has anchored across the singles catalog. The lyrical themes are explicit ("I'm not going to write your typical love song" is the producer's own framing from earlier in the catalog's development), and the album reads as a sustained statement of that perspective rather than as a set of disconnected mood pieces.

What's notable about the sequencing is that the album rewards being listened to in full. Most contemporary electronic releases are structurally designed for single-track extraction — tracks land as individually-legible cuts, and the album frame is a packaging convenience rather than a structural argument. A Grave Marked Strange is built more like a singer-songwriter album in its sequencing logic: the tracks accumulate emotional weight as the runtime progresses, and the album's strongest moments are the ones that arrive after the listener has been inside the record's working register for forty minutes.

What This Means

The structural significance of A Grave Marked Strange, beyond the album itself, is that it demonstrates a working model for how producers operating at the intersection of multiple scenes can produce coherent album-length statements without committing fully to any single scene's working aesthetic. The album is recognizably adjacent to angelcore (the Deathwish collaboration positions it inside the scene's collaboration network); it is recognizably adjacent to witch house (the production palette pulls from the same lineage); it is recognizably adjacent to alt-pop (the vocal performance and lyrical content read in that register). What it doesn't do is fully inhabit any of these categories — and the partial inhabitation is the choice that makes the album distinctive.

For producers thinking about how to position projects at the intersection of contemporary electronic scenes without getting absorbed into any single one of them, A Grave Marked Strange is a useful working example. The album succeeds by treating the genre adjacencies as compositional resources rather than as identity choices — pulling what's structurally useful from each reference point while letting the project's own working voice remain the organizing center.

Where to Start

  • The full album, in sequence. The structural argument depends on the runtime; single-track extraction loses most of what the album does.
  • Eternal Embrace. If a single-track entry point is necessary; the cleanest demonstration of the album's working register.
  • Deathwish (with akiaura and LONOWN, 2025). The collaboration that positioned Baby Jane inside the angelcore scene; useful for context.
  • The A Grave Marked Strange (Sped Up) edition. The producer's own parallel-version reissue; the same material at faster tempo, the differences are instructive.
